# 10 Stoic Rules to Stay Calm Under Pressure

## Cold open (0:00–0:40)

*(VISUAL: Roman legion camp at night, torches, rain. Slow push-in on a lone commander's tent.)*

In the winter of the year 170, the Roman Empire was on fire.

Plague had killed millions. Germanic tribes had crossed the Danube. The treasury was so empty that the emperor auctioned off the palace furniture to pay his soldiers.

And at night, in a cold tent on the frontier, that emperor sat down and wrote notes to himself. Not orders. Not speeches. Reminders — on how to stay calm when everything is falling apart.

His name was Marcus Aurelius. Those notes became *Meditations*.

**[TEXT: 10 Stoic Rules to Stay Calm Under Pressure]**

Today, we'll turn his words, and the words of two other Stoic masters, into ten rules you can use the next time pressure hits. Whether it's a crisis at work, a hard conversation, or a night when your mind won't stop.

---

## Rule 1 — Separate what you control from what you don't (0:40–1:50)

**[TEXT: Rule 1 — Know what is yours to control]**

*(VISUAL: Epictetus, an older man with a cane, teaching students in a sunlit stone courtyard.)*

Epictetus was born a slave. According to one ancient story, his master once twisted his leg until it broke. He later became one of the most respected teachers in the Roman world.

His whole philosophy begins with one sentence:

**[TEXT: "Some things are within our power, while others are not." — Epictetus]**

Your judgments, your choices, your effort, your response. Those are yours. The economy, other people's opinions, the weather, the past. Those are not.

Pressure comes from mixing the two. We pour our energy into what we can't change, and neglect what we can.

So when pressure rises, draw a line down the middle of a page. On the left: what I control. On the right: what I don't. Then give everything you have to the left side, and let the right side go.

---

## Rule 2 — Pause before you react (1:50–3:00)

**[TEXT: Rule 2 — Pause before you react]**

*(VISUAL: Seneca writing by oil lamp; slow candle flicker.)*

Seneca advised an emperor, ran a fortune, and saw more politics than most of us ever will. His advice on anger was simple:

**[TEXT: "The greatest remedy for anger is delay." — Seneca]**

The first reaction is rarely the wisest one. The email you write in the first five minutes is usually the one you regret.

A leader who pauses doesn't look weak. They look in command. Count to ten. Take one slow breath. Say, "Let me think about that." Then respond, once your judgment has caught up with your emotions.

---

## Rule 3 — It's not the event, it's your judgment of it (3:00–4:10)

**[TEXT: Rule 3 — Question your first story]**

*(VISUAL: A marble statue's face in shifting light — half shadow, half sun.)*

**[TEXT: "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the opinions about things." — Epictetus]**

A deal falls through. One person says, "I'm finished." Another says, "Now I know what didn't work." Same event. Two completely different nights of sleep.

Marcus Aurelius put it this way: when something external distresses you, the pain comes from your estimate of it, and that estimate you can revoke at any moment.

Next time you feel the pressure rising, ask: What actually happened? And what story am I adding to it?

---

## Rule 4 — Rehearse the worst in advance (4:10–5:20)

**[TEXT: Rule 4 — Rehearse the worst]**

*(VISUAL: A Roman general studying a map by lamplight before battle.)*

**[TEXT: "We suffer more often in imagination than in reality." — Seneca]**

The Stoics had a practice called *premeditatio malorum*, the premeditation of evils. Before a hard day, they'd quietly imagine what could go wrong.

Not to worry. To prepare.

If the investor says no, what's my next move? If the project fails, what will I still have? When you've already walked through the worst case in your mind, reality loses its power to shock you. You've been there before. You're calm because you're ready.

---

## Rule 5 — Expect difficult people (5:20–6:30)

**[TEXT: Rule 5 — Expect difficult people]**

*(VISUAL: The Roman Senate, crowded and tense; senators arguing.)*

Every morning, Marcus Aurelius prepared himself with a reminder. Today, he told himself, I'll meet people who are meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, and dishonest.

That sounds cynical. It isn't. It's armor.

When you expect difficult people, they stop surprising you. And when they stop surprising you, they stop controlling you. You can deal with them calmly, even kindly, because you saw them coming.

---

## Rule 6 — Do less, but do what matters (6:30–7:30)

**[TEXT: Rule 6 — Do less, better]**

*(VISUAL: A single Roman column standing in an empty field at dawn.)*

Marcus wrote that if you want peace of mind, do fewer things, and ask of each one: is this necessary?

Most pressure isn't caused by one big problem. It's caused by fifty small ones fighting for attention.

So under pressure, simplify. What are the one or two things that truly matter today? Do those well, and let the rest wait.

---

## Rule 7 — Slow down your body to slow down your mind (7:30–8:30)

**[TEXT: Rule 7 — Command your body first]**

*(VISUAL: An emperor walking slowly through a colonnade, hands behind his back.)*

Marcus admired his adoptive father, Emperor Antoninus Pius, above almost anyone. What stood out? His calm. He was never rushed, never harsh, never flustered, even under the weight of an empire.

Calm is not only a thought. It's a posture.

Lower your voice. Slow your speech. Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Your mind follows your body. And the people around you follow your calm.

---

## Rule 8 — The obstacle is the way (8:30–9:40)

**[TEXT: Rule 8 — Use the obstacle]**

*(VISUAL: A river flowing around a boulder; then Roman engineers building a bridge.)*

**[TEXT: "The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way." — Marcus Aurelius]**

When a plan breaks, the Stoic doesn't ask, "Why me?" He asks, "What does this make possible?"

A lost client teaches you what your offer is missing. A setback forces you to become more creative, more patient, more resilient. The obstacle isn't blocking your path. It is your path.

## Rule 9 — Retreat into yourself (9:40–10:50)

**[TEXT: Rule 9 — Build your inner citadel]**

*(VISUAL: A quiet courtyard garden with a small fountain; evening light.)*

Wealthy Romans escaped to villas by the sea or in the mountains. Marcus said you don't need to.

**[TEXT: "Nowhere can man find a quieter or more untroubled retreat than in his own soul." — Marcus Aurelius]**

Five minutes of silence before a meeting. A short walk without your phone. A few lines in a journal at night, exactly as Marcus did.

You can't always leave the storm. But you can always build a quiet place inside it.

---

## Rule 10 — Remember that you will die (10:50–12:00)

**[TEXT: Rule 10 — Memento mori]**

*(VISUAL: A Roman triumph procession; a servant standing behind the victorious general.)*

In ancient Rome, it's said that during a victory parade, a servant stood behind the triumphant general, whispering: "Remember, you are only a man."

Marcus reminded himself that he could leave life at any moment, and let that shape what he did, said, and thought.

This isn't morbid. It's clarifying. The email that feels like a crisis, the argument that feels so important. Will any of it matter in a year? In ten?

When you remember how short life is, most pressure shrinks to its true size.

*(VISUAL: Marcus Aurelius writing in his tent at dawn; the rain has stopped.)*

Marcus Aurelius never got a calm world. He got plague, war, and betrayal. What he built was a calm mind.
